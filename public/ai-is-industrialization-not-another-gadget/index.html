AI is closer to industrialization than to any other technology

Most people compare AI to the internet, the smartphone or the cloud. I think that is the wrong shelf. After reading Power and Progress by Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson, I am convinced the better comparison is industrialization: a change in how work itself gets done, not a new tool on top of old work.

Why the internet is the wrong comparison

The internet, the phone and the cloud mostly changed how we reach work: how we sell, talk and store things. The jobs stayed. A salesperson with email was still a salesperson. AI is different. It does the work: the drafting, the follow-up, the forms, the bookkeeping. That is what machines did to spinning and weaving in the first Industrial Revolution. The task moved from the person to the machine.

What the book says about industrialization

Acemoglu and Johnson's main point is that new technology does not lift everyone by default. They call the hopeful idea the "productivity bandwagon": machines make workers more productive, firms hire more, and wages rise. Their history says the bandwagon only rolls under two conditions. The technology has to make workers more useful, not just replace them. And workers need enough bargaining power to claim a share.

Early British industrialization is their warning. Output grew, but for decades working conditions got worse, wages stayed low and factory surveillance was intense. Machines displaced workers faster than new tasks appeared for them. The gains were shared only later, after the technology changed direction and workers built countervailing power through unions and reform.

They also name the trap: "so-so automation". That is automation that replaces people but adds little real productivity, like a self-checkout that moves the work to the customer. It hurts workers and barely helps anyone else.

Why AI fits that pattern

  • It replaces tasks, not just channels. Like the loom, it takes over work people were paid to do.
  • It spreads across every industry at once. Steam and electricity touched everything. So does AI: law, trucking, insurance, accounting, support.
  • The direction is a choice. The book's strongest idea is that technology has no fixed path. The same AI can be built to cut people or to make each person able to do far more.

What I take from it as a builder

At Dooza we build AI employees for small businesses. The book changed how I think about that. The easy product is so-so automation: replace a person with a bot that is worse but cheaper. The better product makes a small team able to do what only big companies could do before, and keeps a human approving what matters. That is the side of the industrialization story I want to be on.

If AI is industrialization, then the real question is not whether it will be big. It will. The question is who it is built for, and how fast the gains reach the people doing the work. Last time that took decades. I do not think we should wait that long again.
