The spreadsheet killed a job. Then it grew one.

In the late 1970s, an accountant in Boston named Allen Sneider spent whole days on one tweak to a financial model. Change one number and you redo the grid by hand. NPR's Planet Money told his story. I keep thinking about it, because I think small business is about to live through the same thing.

The true story

VisiCalc was "originally released for the Apple II by VisiCorp on October 17, 1979." A Harvard Business School student, Dan Bricklin, had the idea. Sneider became its first registered owner. NPR says the program "could do in seconds what it used to take a person an entire day to do."

Then came the part everyone expects. NPR: "lots of bookkeepers and accounting clerks were replaced by spreadsheet software."

Then came the part few people expect. NPR: "But the number of jobs for accountants? Surprisingly, that actually increased." And: "Accountants became more valuable."

Why it happened

The spreadsheet did not remove the need for accounting. It made one thing cheap: asking "what if?" NPR says asking what if "has become so easy that we manically do it all day long." When a question gets cheap, people ask a lot more of them. Someone has to judge the answers. That someone was an accountant.

The task of recalculating moved to the machine. The judgment stayed with people. And there was more judgment to do.

It is still going on

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics shows both halves today. There were 1,532,400 bookkeeping, accounting and auditing clerk jobs in 2025, and their employment "is projected to decline 6 percent from 2025 to 2035" (BLS).

There were 1,595,200 accountant and auditor jobs in 2025, and their employment "is projected to grow 5 percent from 2025 to 2035, faster than the average for all occupations" (BLS).

Forty-six years on, the clerk job keeps shrinking and the judgment job keeps growing. The change took decades, not a year.

What it predicts for small business

I build AI agents for small businesses, so I read this as a map.

The clerk work of the front office is the first to move. Answering the same question again. Typing a lead into a list. Chasing a quote. Sending the reminder. That is the "recalculate the grid" work of 2026.

What gets cheap is the follow-up. A shop that could never afford to reply to every enquiry in five minutes now can. When that gets cheap, the owner will want more of it, not less. More leads touched, more quotes chased, more customers asked how it went. Then someone has to decide who to call, what to offer and when to say no. That is the new accountant.

I should be fair about the limits. Spreadsheets were used by firms with accountants. Small businesses are earlier. The Census Bureau says "Less than 20% of firms with four or fewer employees reported using AI" in data collected from 14 Dec 2025 to 3 May 2026. Most small firms have not started.

I also do not know that AI agents will follow the spreadsheet path. They do more than arithmetic. They write, talk and decide small things. This could go the other way, and fewer judgment jobs could appear. The book I wrote about last week, Power and Progress, says gains are shared only when the technology makes workers more useful, not just cheaper to replace. That is a choice.

Three predictions I will be graded on

These are my predictions, not facts. I will check them and post the result.

  1. By 31 Dec 2028, the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook page for bookkeeping, accounting and auditing clerks will project a steeper decline than today's 6 percent, and the page for accountants and auditors will still project growth.
  2. By 31 Dec 2028, the Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey will show AI use above 40% for firms with four or fewer employees. Today it is under 20%.
  3. By 31 Dec 2029, answering every customer within minutes, around the clock, will be normal enough for small businesses that customers stop thanking them for it. I will grade this by checking whether a "reply within 5 minutes" promise is a standard line on small-business sites in three industries I sample. This is the soft one, and I will say so when I grade it.
